## When we actually cross singularity, we may not notice at all.

An event horizon is a physically irreversible boundary, a point of no return, often described as the edge of a black hole. What makes it fascinating is that the one crossing it does not know they have crossed it, but only an outside observer can tell where the edge lies.

When we talk about the AI singularity, instead of an exponential curve, I visualise an event horizon. An event horizon pulled by a social scientific gravity: human desire, curiosity, geopolitical rivalry, the urge to be first, the ambition to leave one's name in history.

Because of this, we will neither define the singularity cleanly in scientific terms nor recognise the exact moment we cross it. Only after we are drawn far deeper inside will we realise that we have already passed the point of no return. Some might say that AI became this capable "all of a sudden." No. There is no "all of a sudden." The signals were there every single day, and we simply chose to ignore them.

Recently in South Korea, it came to light that AI has already infiltrated the CSAT (_Suneung_) and various professional licensing exams. Uncomfortable as it is to say, this was entirely predictable. In Korea, far too much of a person's life hinges on a single exam. The incentive is overwhelming, and the technology has caught up. Ultimately, we are moving toward becoming cyborgs, whether loosely coupled or fully integrated. Wearables were already arriving. Warnings and regulatory debates around hidden cameras and networked micro cameras have been circulating for years. Now those sensors are being connected to the body, smart glasses and lenses are already emerging, and all it takes is pairing them with haptic feedback and similar interfaces. Experts must have known, at the very latest, since last year. Yet people act as though it happened "suddenly" this year. Again, we simply chose to ignore them.

In that sense, I believe we have already crossed the AI event horizon in a social science perspective. Once trust erodes, and once meritocracy and the ethos of hard work begin to collapse, leaving people either feeling FOMO without AI or feeling powerless to achieve anything without it, exams as we know them cannot endure.

It's not only about exams. It's also about education and work. And it leads to life, culture, economy. We need a buffer that moves in advance that domino effect.

Rather than being dragged passively behind a technological explosion, we must proactively discuss and redefine our policies, our laws, and the very architecture of our time, the familiar rhythm where life is divided into a third for work, a third for sleep, and a third for everything else, structured around five days of work and two days of rest.

We need a new social structure. In a society built on capital and human labour, the moment AI generates the wealth and superintelligence arrives, humans risk being reduced to something obsolete.

Instead of simply being shaped by technology, we need to ask a more fundamental question:

Using this technology we claim is "liberating," what kind of human life do we actually want to create?
